P68 [Normal] ABSTRACT
P69 [Normal] This project trains a skin-lesion classifier on the HAM10000  dermoscopy dataset, then asks a blunt question: does that success survive contact with Indian skin images?Six ImageNet-pretrained models got fine-tuned. Then they were bundled into an ensemble, with the final mix of weights picked using validation performance. On the HAM10000  test split, the numbers look solid—macro-F lands at ., accuracy at .%.Now comes the stress test. No retraining. The same models get pushed onto DermaCon-IN: , clinical photos of Indian patients, split by patient so leakage doesn’t sneak in. And the wheels start to wobble. On  non-cancer test images, the ensemble still shouts “cancer” .% of the time. Worse, it barely catches the cancers that actually exist in that Indian set. A simple fix helps, at least on the alarms. Add a two-stage setup: a gate trained on Indian data first, then the classifier. That cuts the-cancer rate down to .%.They also rerun experiments across three random seeds and poke at all the usual suspects—class imbalance, skin-tone augmentation, lesion cropping, image corruptions, even how good the explanations look. The takeaway is uncomfortable but clear. High accuracy on HAM10000  doesn’t automatically transfer to Indian clinical photographs. If you want reliable performance there, you need Indian images with biopsy-confirmed cancers.‍‍​‌​​‌​​​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌‌​​​​​‌‌​​‌​​​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌‌‌​​​‌‌‌​​​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​

P78 [Normal] CHAPTER 1
P79 [Heading 1] INTRODUCTION
P80 [Heading 2] 1.1 Background
P81 [Normal] Skin cancer usually gets spotted the old-fashioned way: a clinician looks closely at the skin, often through a dermoscope, and then a biopsy settles it.Melanoma is the one that really scares people. Basal cell and squamous cell cancers show up more often, but they tend to move slower. Now here’s where it gets interesting. Deep neural networks, trained on huge piles of images, can sort skin lesions with performance that lands surprisingly close to dermatologists—at least on carefully curated test sets (Esteva et al., ). Add widely used public datasets like HAM10000  (Tschandl et al., ) and the ISIC challenge collections (Codella et al., ), and suddenly this became a go-to topic for student projects and research labs everywhere.But there’s a catch. These datasets are mostly dermoscopic images pulled from clinics in Europe, Australia, and the United States, where a large share of patients have lighter skin tones (Adamson and Smith, ; Kinyanjui et al., ). So what happens when you take a model raised on that world and drop it into another one—darker skin, or everyday phone photos instead of dermoscopy? You can’t just assume it behaves the same. Evidence suggests it may not (Groh et al., ; Daneshjou et al., ).‍‍​‌​​‌​​​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌‌​​​​​‌‌​​‌​​​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌‌‌​​​‌‌‌​​​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​
P82 [Heading 2] 1.2 Motivation: the review comment
P83 [Normal]  The project started out pretty straightforward: train a handful of models on HAM00, bundle them into an ensemble, bolt on Grad-CAM so the predictions aren’t a total black box, then ship the whole thing as a web app. Clean. Practical. Done.Then the review landed. And yeah—fair point. HAM10000  classification? People have been doing that forever. The feedback was blunt: if this is going to matter, it needs to speak to Indian skin.So this report does both. It lays out the original HAM00 pipeline, and it also digs into what that exact system does when you feed it Indian skin images from the DermaCon-IN dataset (Madarkar et al., ).‍‍​‌​​‌​​​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌‌​​​​​‌‌​​‌​​​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌‌‌​​​‌‌‌​​​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​
P84 [Heading 2] 1.3 Problem statement
P85 [Normal] A model trained on the seven HAM10000  labels has one glaring blind spot: there’s no button for “none of the above.” So hand it a photo of a fungal rash, eczema, or vitiligo, and it still has to slap on one of those seven tumour tags. No choice. And when it lands on melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, or actinic keratosis? That’s a bogus cancer alert.That’s what this work digs into. How often does this misfire happen on Indian skin, in real terms? Can we tamp it down with a practical fix? And once you rerun the whole pipeline over and over—then wrap the results in confidence intervals—which training decisions actually move the needle, instead of just looking good once by luck?‍‍​‌​​‌​​​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌‌​​​​​‌‌​​‌​​​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌‌‌​​​‌‌‌​​​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​
P86 [Heading 2] 1.4 Objectives
P87 [Normal] Train six different transfer-learning setups, then pit them against each other—plus a validation-weighted ensemble—using HAM10000 with a strict lesion-level split.
P88 [Normal] Then comes the real test. Take those same models and run them on Indian clinical photos (DermaCon-IN) as-is. No retraining. 
P89 [Normal] Track how often they incorrectly flag “cancer,” and slice that error rate by disease category and by skin tone.Next, put a gate in front. A two-step system: a lightweight filter trained on Indian data decides which images are even allowed to reach the cancer classifier. Simple idea.
P90 [Normal]  High stakes.After that, dig into all the messy details people love to ignore: class-imbalance tricks, skin-tone augmentation, segmentation plus smart cropping, how the models behave under image corruptions, and whether Grad-CAM is actually telling the truth. Run each experiment with three seeds.
P91 [Normal]  Report confidence intervals. No cherry-picking.Finally, ship something usable: a web app that shows the model’s prediction, draws the lesion boundary, overlays the Grad-CAM heatmap, and throws warnings when the results suggest you should be skeptical.‍‍​‌​​‌​​​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌‌​​​​​‌‌​​‌​​​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌‌‌​​​‌‌‌​​​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​.
P92 [Heading 2] 1.5 Rules followed in this work
P93 [Normal] • No test data was ever used to select a model, hyperparameter, threshold, or ensemble weight; all selections were made using validation data. 
• All training experiments for the skin study were performed using seeds 0, 1 and 2 and are reported as mean ± standard deviation. 
• 95% confidence intervals based on 1,000 bootstrap resamples (either by lesion or by patient) or Wilson intervals. 
• All numbers in this report are taken from a results file created by an executed notebook. The connection is, in paper
• Weak or negative results are reported as such.

P96 [Heading 1] LITERATURE REVIEW
P97 [Heading 2] 2.1 Skin lesion classification on dermoscopic datasets
P98 [Heading 3] Esteva et al. built a convolutional neural network based on clinical and dermoscopic images and achieved dermatologist-level performance on a biopsy-proven test set (Esteva et al., 2017). HAM10 (Tschandl et al., 2018) has dermoscopic images with seven classes and lesion IDs. It was used as a reference standard in the ISIC 2018 challenge (Codella et al., 2019). The following documents, selected for the first review, are summarized in our own terminology; the figures are those given by the authors and checked against the published abstracts whenever possible.
P99 [Heading 3] 2.1.1 Tahir et al. (2023)
P100 [Heading 3] DSCC_Net: Deep learning models for multi-class classification of skin cancer diagnosis using dermoscopic imagery (Cancers). DSCC_Net is a convolutional neural network designed for four skin cancer categories (melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanocytic nevus) and trained on images from ISIC 2020, HAM10, and DermIS. Minority classes were balanced by SMOTE Tomek and the network was tested against six traditional backbones, including EfficientNet-B0. The aggregated test dataset shows 94.17% precision, 99.43% area under the curve and 93.76% sensitivity. Advantages: Clear handling of class imbalance; re-evaluation of six baselines. Limitations: Aggregated datasets make it difficult to compare results; only 4 categories available.
P101 [Heading 3] 2.1.2 Hajiarbabi (2023)
P102 [Normal] Detection of skin cancer using multi-scale deep learning and transfer learning (Journal of Medical Artificial Intelligence). This work detects melanoma using transfer learning with MobileNetV2 at different scales: the full image and two central crops are classified independently and their results aggregated. We could not verify the reported metrics against the publication and therefore do not report them [VERIFY]. Advantages: The model focuses on the lesion via crops; It employs a lightweight backbone. Limitations: Only for binary tasks, 3 forward passes for each image.
P103 [Normal] 
P104 [Heading 3] 2.1.3 Radhika and Chandana (2023)
P105 [Normal] A PeerJ Computer Science study uses MSCDNet to classify skin cancer from dermoscopy images. DenseUNet handles the segmentation in MSCDNet, the binary dragonfly algorithm picks the features, and SqueezeNet does the classification, all on the ISIC 2019 dataset. We couldn't check the reported numbers against the paper, so they're not included here. First, the system segments the background, then it classifies everything. It's got a lot of separate steps that need tuning, so it uses more power..
P106 [Heading 3] 2.1.4 Ali et al. (2023)
P107 [Normal] Enhancing skin lesion detection: a multistage multiclass CNN based framework (Bioengineering). A two stage CNN which first separates the benign from the malignant and then assigns the subclass, using the first stage weights via transfer learning on the ISIC 2018. Result: 93.4% accuracy on the benign/malignant stage and 96.2% on the subclass stage. Pros: The staging does improve the subclass accuracy. Cons: You can't fix the errors from the first stage in the second stage.
P108 [Heading 3] 2.1.5 Chaturvedi et al. (2020)
P109 [Normal] Skin lesion analyser: An efficient seven-way multi-class skin cancer classification using MobileNet (Advanced Machine Learning Technologies and Applications (AMLTA 2020), Advances in Intelligent Systems and Computing, vol. 1141, Springer). A MobileNet pre-trained on ImageNet was fine tuned for the seven HAM10000 classes and deployed as a public web app. Result: 83.1% categorical accuracy and a weighted F1-score of 0.83. Pros: Very lightweight, deployed as a web app. Cons: Lower accuracy than the larger backbones.
P110 [Heading 3] 2.1.6 Al Mahmud et al. (2024)
P111 [Normal] Comparison of the performance of two deep learning models for the classification of skin lesions using an image resampling technique for data augmentation (Proc. SPIE 13137, Applications of Digital Image Processing XLVII). VGG16 and ResNet50 were both fine-tuned on HAM10000 with the classes balanced by random resampling and compared directly. Result: 93.45% accuracy for VGG16 and 94.69% for ResNet50 (VGG16 trained much faster). Pros: A direct comparison of the two backbones on one dataset. Cons: ResNet50 is by a long way the slower to train; per-class results are not given in the abstract.
P112 [Heading 3] 2.1.7 Islam and Ferdouse (2026)
P113 [Normal] Uncertainty-aware and explainable ensemble deep learning framework for multi-class skin lesion classification (arXiv preprint). An ensemble of MaxViT-Tiny ConvNeXt- EfficientNetV2-B0 is used with Monte Carlo dropout to estimate uncertainty and with Grad-CAM++ to provide explanations tested on HAM10000. The result shows 96% accuracy 99% ROC-AUC and 95% macro-F1 after removing predictions. Strengths include both uncertainty estimates and model explanations, in one system. Weaknesses are that the performance numbers only reflect the predictions that were kept the work is a preprint. The ensemble approach is expensive.
P114 [Heading 3] 2.1.8 Sravani and Koppu (2026)
P115 [Normal] Multi-paradigm Vision Transformer ensemble with attention and MLP meta-fusion for explainable dermoscopic skin lesion classification (Frontiers in Medicine). Three transformers (Swin-Tiny ViT-Base and DeiT-Small) are combined with an attention module and an MLP meta-learner on the HAM10000 dataset. The model is trained using class-weighted loss and MixUp. The results show an accuracy of 98.37%, a weighted F1-score of 98.39% and a mean AUC of 0.999. Strengths: This model achieves the reported accuracy, among all the reviewed papers. Weaknesses: The model is heavy and training and deployment are costly.
P116 [Heading 3] 2.1.9 Subbayamma et al. (2026)
P117 [Normal] Transfer Learning Based Multi-Class Skin Lesion Classification Using the HAM10000 Dataset with a Deep Convolutional Network (VGG16). The VGG16 was fine-tuned with frozen early layers and trained on the HAM10000 dataset with class balance and data augmentation techniques. This achieved an overall accuracy of 94.2% and macro-F1 score of 0.88. The model is simple and quick to train, however baselines are not re-run but taken from literature.
P118 [Heading 3] 2.1.10 Ilyosbekov (2025)
P119 [Normal] MelanomaNet: An explainable deep learning model for skin lesion classification (arXiv preprint). This uses an EfficientNetV2 as it’s backbone and implements Grad-CAM++ techniques, automatic ABCDE criteria, concept activation vectors, and Monte Carlo dropout. With 9 classes from the ISIC 2019 dataset. The model achieved an accuracy of 85.61% and weighted F1-score of 0.8564. The benefits were multiple explanations that are complementary and related to the clinical criteria. The limitations were that it didn’t achieve as high an accuracy as CNN only and is a preprint.

P123 [Heading 2] 2.3 Skin tone, imaging type and fairness
P124 [Normal] Adamson and Smith (Adamson and Smith, 2018) pointed out that dermatology AI trained mainly on light skin may perform worse on darker skin and widen existing gaps in care. Kinyanjui et al. (Kinyanjui et al., 2020) estimated the skin tone of images in public dermoscopic datasets and found that dark skin is rare in them. Groh et al. (Groh et al., 2021) built the Fitzpatrick 17k collection of clinical images labelled with Fitzpatrick skin type (Fitzpatrick, 1988) and showed that accuracy changes when models are trained on some skin types and tested on others. Daneshjou et al. (Daneshjou et al., 2022) curated the DDI set of biopsy-confirmed clinical images with many darker-skin cases and found that published classifiers performed worse on it than on their original test data, especially on dark skin. The Monk skin tone scale (Monk, 2023) offers a finer ten-point description of skin tone than the Fitzpatrick types.
P125 [Heading 2] 2.4 Indian skin data
P126 [Normal] DermaCon-IN (Madarkar et al., 2025) is a dataset of clinical photographs of Indian skin disorders collected in South India. Each image has a main class, a sub-class and a diagnosis, together with Fitzpatrick and Monk skin-tone labels. Most images show infections, inflammatory and pigmentary disorders; tumours are a small minority. We found no earlier work that tested HAM10000-trained cancer classifiers on it.
P127 [Heading 2] 2.5 Methods used in this work
P128 [Normal] The backbones are EfficientNet (Tan and Le, 2019), ResNet (He et al., 2016), DenseNet (Huang et al., 2017), ConvNeXt (Liu et al., 2022) and the Swin Transformer (Liu et al., 2021), all pretrained on ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009). Hair is removed with a DullRazor-style filter (Lee et al., 1997) and contrast is equalised with CLAHE (Zuiderveld, 1994). Focal loss (Lin et al., 2017), Shades of Grey colour constancy (Finlayson and Trezzi, 2004) and U-Net segmentation (Ronneberger et al., 2015) are used in the supporting studies. Explanations use Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017) and are scored with deletion and insertion curves (Petsiuk et al., 2018); robustness tests follow the corruption benchmark idea of Hendrycks and Dietterich (Hendrycks and Dietterich, 2019). Confidence intervals use the bootstrap (Efron, 1979) and the Wilson interval (Wilson, 1927).
P129 [Heading 2] 2.6 Observations from the literature
P130 [Normal] Transfer learning from ImageNet is the common starting point, and both CNNs and transformers are used.
P131 [Normal] Class imbalance is handled by resampling, class weights or focal loss; accuracy alone hides poor results on rare classes.
P132 [Normal] Explanation maps such as Grad-CAM are often shown, but rarely tested for whether they point at what the model uses.
P133 [Normal] Several works add a segmentation or cropping step, or split the task into stages.
P134 [Normal] Ensembles report the highest numbers but cost more to run.
P135 [Heading 2] 2.7 Limitations of the literature and how this work responds
P136 [Normal] Results are reported on different datasets, class sets and splits, so headline numbers cannot be compared. Here: all models are compared on one lesion-level split, with confidence intervals.
P137 [Normal] Most studies use one training run. Here: the supporting studies use three seeds.
P138 [Normal] Skin tone and non-tumour conditions are rarely tested. Here: the models are tested on Indian clinical images, including thousands of non-tumour conditions.
P139 [Normal] Explanations are seldom evaluated. Here: Grad-CAM maps are scored against random maps.
P140 [Normal] Selection on test data is not always ruled out. Here: every choice is made on validation data, and automatic sanity checks confirm it.

P183 [Heading 2] 4.2 Preprocessing
P184 [Normal] Every image, from any dataset, goes through the same steps (Figure 4.2). Hair is found with a black-hat morphological filter (kernel 17 pixels, threshold 12, keeping only long thin components) and removed by inpainting with radius 3 (Lee et al., 1997). Contrast is equalised with CLAHE (clip limit 2.0, 8 × 8 tiles) (Zuiderveld, 1994), the image is resized to 224 × 224 pixels and normalised with the ImageNet mean and standard deviation.

P187 [Heading 2] 4.3 Models and training on HAM10000
P188 [Normal] Five architectures pretrained on ImageNet were fine-tuned with a new classification head (dropout followed by a linear layer with seven outputs). EfficientNet-B0 was trained twice, the second time for longer. Training images are randomly cropped (scale 0.75–1.0), flipped horizontally and vertically, rotated by up to 30 degrees and colour-jittered. The loss is cross-entropy with square-rooted balanced class weights and label smoothing. The checkpoint with the best validation macro-F1 is kept.

P191 [Heading 2] 4.4 Ensemble and test-time augmentation
P192 [Normal] Each model averages its softmax output over three views: the original image and its horizontal and vertical flips. The ensemble averages the six models with weights; the weights were the best of 4,000 random weight vectors by validation macro-F1 (seed 42). An equal-weight average and the best combination of three models were also evaluated. The final choice was the option with the highest validation macro-F1.
P193 [Heading 2] 4.5 Zero-shot evaluation on DermaCon-IN
P194 [Normal] The HAM10000 models and the ensemble were applied to the DermaCon-IN test images without retraining, after the same preprocessing. An image is non-neoplastic when its main class is not “Neoplasms and tumors”. The false-cancer rate (FCR) is the share of non-neoplastic images whose top predicted class is mel, bcc or akiec. The FCR was computed overall, per disease group, per Fitzpatrick group and per Monk group. Accuracy on the mapped images and detection of the malignant images were secondary measures.
P195 [Heading 2] 4.6 Two-stage pipeline
P196 [Normal] Stage 1 is an EfficientNet-B0 trained on the 8 DermaCon-IN main classes with the HAM10000 recipe and square-rooted class weights, with three seeds. Stage 2 is the HAM10000 ensemble. An image goes to Stage 2 only when its Stage 1 neoplasm probability reaches a threshold t. For each seed, t is the highest value that keeps at least 90% of the validation neoplasm images; the test set was not used.
P197 [Heading 2] 4.7 Supporting studies on HAM10000
P198 [Normal] All supporting studies use EfficientNet-B0 with the HAM10000 recipe, seeds 0, 1 and 2, and the same lesion-level split. The study models are evaluated on single views (no test-time augmentation).

P207 [Heading 3] 4.7.3 Lesion segmentation and cropping
P208 [Normal] A U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) with an ImageNet EfficientNet-B0 encoder was trained on the HAM10000 masks with binary cross-entropy plus Dice loss (three seeds; the checkpoint was chosen by validation Dice). P214 [Heading 3] 4.7.5 Explanation quality
P215 [Normal] Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017) maps were scored with deletion and insertion curves (Petsiuk et al., 2018): pixels are removed (replaced by a blurred copy) or added in order of importance in 20 steps, and the area under the class-probability curve is measured. A good map gives a low deletion score and a high insertion score; a random map is the baseline. 200 test images were used.
P216 [Heading 2] 4.8 Statistics
P217 [Normal] Results of repeated runs are given as mean ± standard deviation over the three seeds. Confidence intervals come from 1,000 bootstrap resamples (Efron, 1979) of lesions (HAM10000) or patients (DermaCon-IN), so that repeated images of one lesion or patient are resampled together. Proportions such as the FCR also get Wilson intervals (Wilson, 1927). Differences between two setups (for example crop vs. no crop) are paired: the same bootstrap resample is used for both.

P315 [Heading 2] 6.10 Discussion
P316 [Normal] The HAM10000 ensemble is a reasonable dermoscopic classifier, but it misses about 43.1% of melanomas even there. On Indian clinical photographs it fails in two ways: it calls 11.0% of common non-tumour conditions cancer, and it does not recognise the few tumours it should know. The false alarms do not depend on Fitzpatrick type within DermaCon-IN, so the main causes appear to be the change from dermoscopy to ordinary photographs and the presence of conditions the model has no class for. A gate trained on Indian data reduces the alarms, which supports the second explanation, but the gate itself is weak.
P317 [Normal] The supporting studies are useful mainly as warnings. A rule fixed in advance chose an imbalance setup that did not win on test; skin-tone augmentation did not help; cropping improved the headline metric while lowering cancer sensitivity; and strong JPEG compression cut cancer detection sharply. Each of these would have looked different with one seed, no confidence intervals, or selection on the test set.
P318 [Heading 2] 6.11 Limitations
P319 [Normal] Clinical images, versus dermoscopic images: DermaCon-IN images are photographs HAM10000 images are dermoscopic. The effects of skin tone and imaging type cannot be separated.
P320 [Normal] few malignant images: 19 in all of DermaCon-IN and 5 (basal cell carcinoma only) in the test split. So cancer sensitivity on skin is essentially unmeasured. There is no melanoma, in the test split.
P321 [Normal]  The mapping from DermaCon-IN, to HAM10000 was created using diagnosis names. Has not been checked by a dermatologist.
P322 [Normal] The gate threshold was adjusted using 10 validation neoplasm images and it changes a lot depending on the seed. Because of this the gate ends up rejecting dermoscopic images.
P323 [Normal] The imbalance studys validation choice had a score, on the test compared to the default. This result is reported ass without any correction.
P324 [Normal] DermaCon-IN does not have images of Fitzpatrick I–II. Has very few images of Monk 8–10 so the comparison of skin tones covers a small range.
P325 [Normal]  DDI could not be obtained so DDI has no darker-skin test that contains biopsy-confirmed cancers..

P327 [Heading 1] CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
P328 [Heading 2] 7.1 Conclusion
P329 [Heading 2] This project trained six learning models and a validation-weighted ensemble for seven-class skin lesion classification on HAM10000 (test macro-F1 0.747) and built a web application around them. Asked to make the work relevant to skin we tested the models on DermaCon-IN without retraining. They called 11.0% of -tumour Indian skin photographs cancer recognised few of the lesion types they were trained on and missed most of the few Indian cancers available. A gate trained on data lowered the false-cancer rate to 5.6%. The supporting studies, each repeated with three seeds showed no benefit from skin-tone augmentation, a trade-off from lesion cropping and a sharp loss of cancer detection under JPEG compression. Good results on HAM10000 do not mean results, for Indian patients.

P379 [Normal] def hair_mask(img_rgb: np.ndarray,
P380 [Normal]               kernel_size: int = config.HAIR_KERNEL_SIZE,
P381 [Normal]               threshold: int = config.HAIR_THRESHOLD,
P382 [Normal]               min_length: int = config.HAIR_MIN_LENGTH):
P383 [Normal]     """Black-hat response and binary hair mask.
P384 [Normal]  
P385 [Normal]     The black-hat transform responds to thin dark structures. Thresholding alone also catches skin
P386 [Normal]     texture and the lesion's pigment network, so only connected components that are long
P387 [Normal]     (bounding box >= min_length px) and sparse (fill ratio < 0.4), i.e. hair-shaped, are kept.
P388 [Normal]     """
P389 [Normal]     gray = cv2.cvtColor(img_rgb, cv2.COLOR_RGB2GRAY)
P390 [Normal]     kernel = cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_CROSS, (kernel_size, kernel_size))
P391 [Normal]     blackhat = cv2.morphologyEx(gray, cv2.MORPH_BLACKHAT, kernel)
P392 [Normal]     _, mask = cv2.threshold(blackhat, threshold, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY)
P393 [Normal]     n, labels, stats, _ = cv2.connectedComponentsWithStats(mask, connectivity=8)
P394 [Normal]     w, h, area = stats[:, cv2.CC_STAT_WIDTH], stats[:, cv2.CC_STAT_HEIGHT], stats[:, cv2.CC_STAT_AREA]
P395 [Normal]     keep = (np.maximum(w, h) >= min_length) & (area / np.maximum(w * h, 1) < 0.4)
P396 [Normal]     keep[0] = False  # background
P397 [Normal]     mask = np.where(keep[labels], 255, 0).astype(np.uint8)
P398 [Normal]     return blackhat, cv2.dilate(mask, np.ones((3, 3), np.uint8), iterations=1)

P466 [Normal] Wilson confidence interval (src/study.py, wilson)
P467 [Normal] def wilson(successes, n, z=1.959964):
P468 [Normal]     """95% Wilson score interval for a proportion."""
P469 [Normal]     if n == 0:
P470 [Normal]         return (float("nan"), float("nan"))
P471 [Normal]     p = successes / n
P472 [Normal]     d = 1 + z ** 2 / n
P473 [Normal]     c = p + z ** 2 / (2 * n)
P474 [Normal]     h = z * np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z ** 2 / (4 * n ** 2))
P475 [Normal]     return (float((c - h) / d), float((c + h) / d))

P477 [Normal] Square crop around the predicted lesion (src/study_extra.py, square_box)
P478 [Normal] def square_box(mask, margin=0.15, min_area=0.01):
P479 [Normal]     """Fractional square (x0, y0, x1, y1) around the largest component, or None if the mask is too small."""
P480 [Normal]     m = largest_component(mask)
P481 [Normal]     if m.mean() < min_area:
P482 [Normal]         return None
P483 [Normal]     ys, xs = np.where(m)
P484 [Normal]     h, w = m.shape
P485 [Normal]     cx, cy = (xs.min() + xs.max() + 1) / 2 / w, (ys.min() + ys.max() + 1) / 2 / h
P486 [Normal]     half = max((xs.max() - xs.min() + 1) / w, (ys.max() - ys.min() + 1) / h) * (0.5 + margin)
P487 [Normal]     half = min(half, 0.5)
P488 [Normal]     cx, cy = min(max(cx, half), 1 - half), min(max(cy, half), 1 - half)
P489 [Normal]     return cx - half, cy - half, cx + half, cy + half
